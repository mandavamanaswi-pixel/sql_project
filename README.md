# ShopSphere: E-Commerce Analytics with SQL

I built this project to practice what a data analyst does day to day: take raw business data, ask useful questions, answer them with SQL, and turn the answers into recommendations a manager could act on.

Everything is written in **MySQL 8**. There are no external files or tools to install. One script builds the database and fills it with data, and another set of scripts runs the analysis.

---

## The story

ShopSphere is a made-up online store. It has customers, products, orders, website visits, and a marketing budget. I imagined being the analyst on its team and answering four questions the business would care about:

1. **How is the business doing overall?** (revenue, profit, best products, best customers)
2. **When should we reach out to customers who stop buying?**
3. **Where do shoppers give up on the website, and did the new checkout page actually help?**
4. **Which marketing channels are worth the money?**

Each question has its own SQL file, and each ends with a clear recommendation.

> **A note on the data:** all of it is synthetic (generated by SQL code, not taken from a real company). I designed realistic patterns into it, such as customers reordering every few weeks and one checkout design being genuinely better, so there would be something real to find. The goal is to show that my SQL finds those patterns correctly. The marketing costs are assumptions I made up, not real market prices.

## What is in the database

| Table | What it holds | Size |
|---|---|---|
| `customers` | Who they are, city, signup date, and how they found the store | 500 |
| `products`, `categories` | What is sold, with price and cost (so profit can be calculated) | 30 products, 6 categories |
| `orders`, `order_items` | Every purchase and its line items; status is completed, cancelled or returned | ~1,400 orders, ~2,750 lines |
| `sessions`, `web_events` | Website visits and what each visitor did (view, add to cart, checkout, purchase) | 30,000 sessions, ~54,000 events |
| `marketing_spend` | Money spent per month on each marketing channel | 70 rows |

```
categories 1──∞ products 1──∞ order_items ∞──1 orders ∞──1 customers
sessions 1──∞ web_events                marketing_spend (month × channel)
```

I also created **views**, which are saved queries. The most important one, `v_order_lines`, calculates revenue and profit per order line in one place (`quantity × price × (1 − discount)`, completed orders only), so I never rewrite that logic.

## What each part does

### 1. Core sales analysis (`04_analysis_queries.sql`)
Twelve queries covering the basics:
- Revenue, average order value and profit margin
- Month-over-month growth and running totals
- Best products in each category
- Customer groups using **RFM** (how recently, how often, and how much each customer buys)
- Whether a small group of customers drives most of the revenue

**Result:** $600K revenue from 1,221 completed orders, a $491 average order, and a 38.5% profit margin. About 64% of buyers purchase more than once, and 48% of buyers generate 80% of revenue.

### 2. When do customers stop buying? (`05_purchase_gap_churn.sql`)
"Churn" means a customer who has stopped buying, and I did not want to guess at a cutoff. Instead I used `LAG()` to measure the number of days between each customer's consecutive orders, then used that pattern to decide what "late" means.

- Half of repeat orders happen within **39 days** of the previous one, 75% within **72 days**, and 90% within **115 days**.
- Customers are labelled **Active**, **At Risk** or **Churned** depending on how long they have been quiet.
- 87.5% of buyers are already churned. The 36 **At Risk** customers hold $95K of lifetime spending, so they are the ones worth saving.
- The file also builds a **cohort table**: customers grouped by the month of their first order, showing what share buys again 1 to 6 months later.

**Recommendation:** send a reminder email at **day 39**, a discount offer at **day 72**, and a final "why did you leave?" message at **day 115**.

### 3. Website funnel and A/B test (`06_funnel_seed.sql`, `07_funnel_ab_analysis.sql`)
A **funnel** shows how many visitors reach each step: visit → view a product → add to cart → start checkout → buy.

- Overall conversion is **5.0%**. The biggest leak is between viewing a product and adding it to the cart (72.5% leave).
- Mobile is 55% of traffic but converts at **3.3%** versus **7.7%** on desktop.

The store also tested a new one-page checkout (**variant B**) against the old one (**variant A**), with visitors split randomly. I checked whether the difference was real or just luck:
- Purchase rate at checkout rose from **55.9% to 65.6%** (+9.7 percentage points).
- A statistical test (z-score 4.91, 95% confidence interval +5.8 to +13.5 points) says this is very unlikely to be chance, and I confirmed the test had enough visitors to trust the result.
- Tablet showed only a small, unreliable difference because there were few tablet checkouts, so I did not claim a win there.

**Recommendation:** roll out variant B, then work on the product page, where the most visitors are lost.

### 4. Are our marketing channels worth it? (`08_marketing_seed.sql`, `09_clv_cac_analysis.sql`)
Two numbers matter here:
- **CAC** (customer acquisition cost): what it costs to win one customer.
- **LTV** (lifetime value): the gross profit that customer brings in during their first 12 months.

If LTV is well above CAC, the channel pays for itself. I compared them for each channel, and also worked out how many months it takes to earn the acquisition cost back.

| Channel | CAC | 12-month LTV | LTV ÷ CAC | Payback | Share of spend | Share of profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Organic | $29 | $705 | 24.0x | month 1 | 4.6% | 28.3% |
| Email | $45 | $425 | 9.4x | month 1 | 8.3% | 20.1% |
| Referral | $98 | $490 | 5.0x | month 1 | 16.2% | 21.0% |
| Social | $153 | $365 | 2.4x | month 2 | 32.3% | 19.9% |
| Paid Search | $245 | $262 | 1.07x | month 9 | **38.6%** | **10.7%** |

**Recommendation:** Paid Search gets the biggest share of the budget but produces the smallest share of profit. I would cap it and shift part of that money into Referral and Email, while checking first whether Paid Search helps other channels convert in ways this data cannot show.

## How to run it

You need MySQL 8.0 or newer.

```bash
./run_all.sh root
```

This builds the database, then runs every analysis. If you prefer a visual tool such as MySQL Workbench or DBeaver, run the files in this order:

1. Build: `01_schema` → `02_seed_data` → `03_views` → `06_funnel_seed` → `08_marketing_seed`
2. Analyze: `04_analysis_queries`, `05_purchase_gap_churn`, `07_funnel_ab_analysis`, `09_clv_cac_analysis`

## SQL skills this project shows

- **Database design:** tables, primary and foreign keys, constraints, indexes, views
- **Joins and aggregation:** multi-table joins, `GROUP BY`, conditional counting
- **CTEs:** multi-step queries that stay readable
- **Window functions:** `LAG`, `RANK`, `NTILE`, `ROW_NUMBER`, running totals
- **Analytics methods:** cohort retention, RFM segmentation, funnel analysis, A/B testing, CAC and LTV
- **Working around MySQL limits:** MySQL has no percentile function, so I calculated percentiles with `ROW_NUMBER()`

## Limitations

- The data is synthetic, so the results show that the method works, not that any real business behaves this way.
- Churn thresholds come from this dataset's own buying rhythm. A different business would need its own.
- LTV is the profit actually earned in the first 12 months, not a forecast of lifetime value.
- Marketing costs are assumed, and the budget-shifting idea assumes returns would stay the same as spending grows, which rarely holds in practice.
